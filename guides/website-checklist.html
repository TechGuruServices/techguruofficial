The 12-Point Local Business Website & Branding Checklist

Most local business websites are quietly losing customers every day. Not because the business is bad — because the website is. This checklist takes about 10 minutes. Run through it on your phone and your computer, and you’ll know exactly where your site stands.

The 12-Point Checklist

1. Loads in under 3 seconds

More than half your visitors are on phones, and they won't wait. If your page takes 5+ seconds to load, roughly 4 out of 10 visitors leave before they see anything. Speed is the single biggest silent killer of local business websites. How to check: Open your site on your phone using mobile data (not Wi-Fi). Count "one-one-thousand, two-one-thousand..." — you should see real content before three.

2. Looks right on a phone

Over 60% of local searches happen on mobile. If text is tiny, buttons overlap, or you have to pinch and zoom, you're losing the majority of your potential customers before they read a word. How to check: Visit every main page on your phone. Can you read everything without zooming? Do buttons and menus work with your thumb?

3. Says what you do within 5 seconds

Visitors decide in seconds whether to stay. If your homepage headline is "Welcome to our website" or just your logo, people bounce. Your headline should say what you do, who you do it for, and where — immediately. How to check: Show your homepage to someone for 5 seconds, then ask: "What does this business do?" If they can't answer, rewrite the headline.

4. Phone number is click-to-call on mobile

When someone's pipe is leaking or their AC dies, they don't fill out a form — they call. If your phone number is just text they can't tap, you've added friction at the exact moment a customer is ready to hire you. How to check: On your phone, tap your phone number. It should offer to call immediately.

5. Google Business Profile is linked

Your Google listing is often the first thing customers see — it shows your hours, reviews, map pin, and phone number. If your website doesn't link to it (and it doesn't link back), you're missing free visibility in local search. How to check: Search your business name on Google. Does a business panel show up on the right? Does your website link to it anywhere?

6. Uses HTTPS (the padlock icon)

If your site shows "Not Secure" in the address bar, visitors get a warning before they even see your content. Browsers flag it, Google ranks you lower, and customers assume the business is outdated or untrustworthy. How to check: Look at the address bar — you should see a padlock icon and "https://" at the start of your URL.

7. Contact form actually works

This one is embarrassingly common: the form looks fine, you hit "send," and the message goes nowhere. No email arrives. No notification. The customer thinks they contacted you, and you never know they exist. How to check: Fill out your own contact form right now. Did you receive the message within a few minutes?

8. Shows real photos, not stock photos

Customers can spot stock photos instantly, and it makes a business feel fake. Real photos of your team, your trucks, your shop, your actual work — that's what builds trust with someone choosing between you and a competitor. How to check: Look at every photo on your site. Would a customer recognize your actual business from these images? If not, replace them.

9. Reviews or testimonials are visible

People trust other customers more than they trust you. If your reviews only live on Google and never appear on your site, visitors have to leave your page to find proof you're good — and they might not come back. How to check: Can a visitor find at least 2-3 customer reviews or testimonials on your site without leaving the page?

10. Every page has a clear next step

Every page should tell the visitor what to do: call now, get a quote, book online. If a page just ends with no call-to-action, you've let a potential customer wander off with no reason to contact you. How to check: Scroll to the bottom of each main page. Is there an obvious button or phone number telling the visitor what to do next?

11. Page titles and descriptions are set

When someone Googles "plumber Missoula," Google shows your page title and a short description. If yours says "Home" or is blank, you look unprofessional in search results — and Google has less reason to rank you. How to check: Google your business name. Does the blue link text describe your business and location, or does it just say "Home"?

12. Analytics is installed

If you don't know how many people visit your site, where they come from, or which pages they leave from, you're flying blind. Free tools take 10 minutes to set up and tell you exactly what's working. How to check: Ask whoever built your site whether Google Analytics (or similar) is installed. If nobody knows, it probably isn't.

Your Score

Count your checkmarks:

  • 10–12: Your site is in great shape. Keep it updated and you're ahead of most local competitors.
  • 7–9: Solid foundation, but the gaps are costing you customers. Fix the misses above — most take under an hour.
  • 4–6: Your website is actively working against you. Prioritize speed, mobile, and the contact form first.
  • 0–3: Your site may be worse than having no site at all — it's giving a bad first impression. Time for a rebuild, not a patch.

Common Mistakes Local Business Sites Make

No clear call-to-action. The site describes the business but never asks the visitor to do anything. No "Call Now" button, no quote form, no booking link — just information and a dead end.

Stock photos only. Smiling strangers in hard hats who've never been to Montana. Customers notice, and it quietly signals that the business has nothing real to show.

Slow loading. Huge uncompressed images, bloated page builders, too many plugins. Every extra second of load time costs real customers, especially on phones.

Not mobile-friendly. The site was designed for a desktop in 2015 and never updated. Text is microscopic, menus don't work, and the phone number can't be tapped.

Outdated hours and info. Holiday hours from two years ago. An old address. A phone number that changed. Nothing erodes trust faster than wrong information.

No reviews anywhere on the site. The business might have 50 five-star Google reviews, but the website shows zero proof. Visitors shouldn't have to go hunting for reassurance.

Broken contact form. It looks fine. It does nothing. Messages vanish into the void, and the business owner has no idea how many leads they've lost.
