Real 2026 Website Pricing, Explained

One builder advertises “$20 a month,” a freelancer quotes $3,000, and an agency sends a proposal for $18,000 — for what looks like the same thing. They’re not selling the same thing. Here’s what each option really costs and what you really get.

What a Website Actually Costs (and Why Nobody Will Tell You Straight)

Website pricing is a mess. One builder advertises "$20 a month," a freelancer quotes $3,000, and an agency sends a proposal for $18,000 — for what looks like the same thing. The truth is, they're not selling the same thing, and nobody in the industry is very motivated to explain the difference. This guide does. No jargon, no sales pitch — just what each option really costs, what you really get, and how to avoid paying for the wrong thing.

The Four Ways to Get a Website in 2026

1. DIY Builders (Wix, Squarespace, GoDaddy) — $20–$60/month + Your Time

What you actually get: A template-based site you build yourself with drag-and-drop tools. Hosting, basic SSL, and a domain connection are usually bundled in. You pick a theme, swap in your text and photos, and publish.

What you don't get: Custom design, real SEO setup, or anything beyond what the template allows. Load times tend to be slow because builders load heavy code on every page. You're also renting, not owning — stop paying and the site is gone.

Who it's right for: Brand-new businesses that need something online this week and have more time than budget. If you're testing whether a business idea works, this is fine. If customers already depend on you, it won't hold up.

Real cost over 3 years: $720–$2,160 in fees, plus 40–80 hours of your time building and maintaining it. At a modest $50/hour value on your time, that's another $2,000–$4,000.

2. Freelancers — $2,000–$8,000 (One-Time)

What you actually get: A custom or semi-custom site built by one person. Typically includes mobile-friendly design, basic on-page SEO, contact forms, and Google Maps integration. The quality varies enormously — a great freelancer at $4,000 can outperform a mediocre agency at $12,000.

What you don't get: Guaranteed availability. Freelancers juggle multiple clients, disappear on vacations, and sometimes vanish mid-project. Ongoing maintenance is usually a handshake deal, not a plan.

Who it's right for: Small businesses with a defined project and a clear idea of what they want. If you can describe your site in a paragraph and don't need complex features, a good freelancer is the best value in the industry.

Watch out for: Freelancers quoting under $1,500 for a "full custom site." At that price, they're either using a template and calling it custom, or they're cutting corners you'll discover in six months.

3. Small Agencies — $5,000–$15,000 (One-Time)

What you actually get: A team — usually a designer, a developer, and someone managing the project. Custom design, copywriting help, real SEO foundation, professional photography direction, and testing across devices and browsers. You get a process: discovery, wireframes, revisions, launch checklist.

What you don't get: Enterprise-level integrations, custom web applications, or dedicated long-term support teams. You're getting a polished marketing site, not a software product.

Who it's right for: Established businesses where the website drives real revenue — the contractor whose phone rings from Google, the retailer whose online catalog matters, the service business competing on trust. If a bad website is costing you customers, this pays for itself.

4. Large Agencies — $15,000–$50,000+ (One-Time)

What you actually get: Strategy workshops, brand development, custom everything, dedicated account managers, and often post-launch marketing support. These firms build for companies where the website is a core business asset.

Who it's right for: Mid-size companies and up, or anyone needing complex functionality — customer portals, e-commerce at scale, multi-location management. For a typical local business, this is overkill. You'd be paying for overhead you don't need.

What Drives the Price Up or Down

Two quotes for a "5-page website" can differ by $10,000. Here's why:

  • Custom design vs. template: A designer building your layout from scratch costs 3–5x more than adapting a premium template. Templates aren't bad — but if every competitor uses the same one, you look interchangeable.
  • Number of pages: Each page needs design, copy, and SEO work. A 5-page brochure site and a 30-page site with service-area pages are different projects.
  • Copywriting: Most business owners underestimate this. Writing clear, persuasive copy for your whole site is a real skill — agencies charge $500–$2,000+ for it, and it's often the difference between a site that looks nice and one that brings in calls.
  • Photography: Stock photos are cheap and look it. Real photos of your team, your shop, your work build trust that stock images can't. Budget $300–$1,500 for a half-day shoot, or do it yourself with a modern phone and good light.
  • Integrations: Online booking, payment processing, email signup automation, CRM connections — each one adds hours. A simple contact form is included in most quotes; a full booking system is not.
  • SEO setup: There's a big difference between "we'll add your keywords" and actual SEO — keyword research, meta structure, local business schema, Google Business Profile alignment, page speed optimization. Real SEO foundation adds $1,000–$3,000 to a project and is worth every penny for local businesses.
  • Timeline: Need it in two weeks? Rush fees are real — expect 25–50% on top. A normal timeline is 3–8 weeks depending on scope.

The Hidden Ongoing Costs (Budget for These)

The project quote isn't the total cost. Every website has running expenses:

CostTypical RangeWhat It Is
Hosting$10–$50/monthWhere your site lives. Cheap shared hosting is slow; managed hosting is faster and handles updates.
Domain~$15/yearYour web address. Renew it yourself and never let anyone else "hold" it for you.
Maintenance & updates$50–$300/monthSoftware updates, security patches, backups, small content changes. Skip this and your site becomes a hacking target within a year.
SSL certificate$0–$100/yearThe padlock in the browser. Most good hosts include it free now — if someone charges you $200/year for SSL, push back.
Backups$0–$20/monthAutomatic copies of your site. Your host may include this; if not, it's cheap insurance.
Email hosting$0–$12/user/monthProfessional email at your domain. Google Workspace and Microsoft 365 are the standards.

Realistic annual running cost for a small business site: $300–$1,500/year, depending on hosting quality and whether you pay for maintenance or handle updates yourself.

Red Flags: When to Walk Away

  • "Full website for $499!" — At that price, you're getting a template with your logo pasted in, built in an afternoon, with no SEO, no strategy, and no support. It will cost you more to fix than to build right.
  • No contract, no timeline. A professional puts the scope, price, timeline, and revision rounds in writing. Verbal agreements on a $5,000 project are how disputes start.
  • You don't own your domain or content. Some builders and cheap providers register your domain under their account. If you ever leave, they hold your web address hostage. Always register the domain yourself.
  • Vague timelines. "We'll get it done soon" isn't a timeline. You want milestones: design draft by X, revisions by Y, launch by Z.
  • No mention of mobile. Over 60% of local searches happen on phones. If a provider isn't leading with mobile-first design in 2026, they're behind.
  • They won't explain what's included. "Everything you need" isn't an answer. Get an itemized list.
